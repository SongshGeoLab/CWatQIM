
## Appendix S3: Agent-based Model ODD+D Protocol

The protocol below follows the Overview, Design concepts, Details (ODD) convention
[@grimm2006], extended with the decision-making block that makes it ODD+D, so that the two stages of the agents' annual choice and the point at which each
stochastic draw occurs are stated explicitly.

### Overview

#### Purpose

The agent-based model (ABM) presented here simulates the coupled human-water system in the Yellow River Basin (YRB) to investigate how water quota institutions shape irrigation water withdrawal decisions and their system-wide consequences. The model is designed to understand the mechanisms through which administrative water quotas, implemented at the provincial level and enforced at the prefecture level, influence the composition of water sources (surface water versus groundwater), irrigation efficiency, and crop productivity. The model enables counterfactual analysis to assess policy effects by comparing scenarios with different enforcement regimes.

The model is designed for river basin scientists, water resource managers, decision-makers, and policy analysts interested in understanding the complex trade-offs arising from water quota institutions in transboundary river basins. It provides a mechanistic framework for analysing how local agencies respond to institutional constraints and how these responses cascade through the coupled human-water system.

#### Entities, State Variables, and Scales

##### Entities

The model contains two types of agents. Prefecture-level agents (N = 59) represent prefecture-level water management agencies that make decisions regarding irrigation water withdrawals. Each prefecture-level agent corresponds to a prefecture-level administrative unit through which the Yellow River flows. These agents are the primary decision-making units that optimise their water source portfolio (surface water and groundwater) to maximise their utility while facing quota constraints.

##### State Variables

Prefecture-level agents maintain several categories of state variables. Water-related variables include the surface water quota allocated from the province (in units of 1e8 m³, hundred million cubic metres), actual surface water and groundwater withdrawals (both in 1e8 m³), total water use (surface plus groundwater, in 1e8 m³), and net irrigation water after accounting for conveyance and on-farm losses (in 1e8 m³). Agricultural variables include irrigation area by crop type (maize, wheat, rice) in hectares, water use intensity by crop type in millimetres, actual crop yield by crop type in tonnes per hectare, and potential crop yield by crop type.

Behavioural traits include boldness, which represents the probability of choosing to defect (exceed quota) and ranges from 0 to 1, vengefulness, which represents sensitivity to others' non-compliance and ranges from 0 to 1, and willing, which is the current decision intention, either "C" (compliance) or "D" (defect).

Performance metrics include the economic payoff (revenue minus water costs), the gross crop revenue (collected for analysis; it does not enter the utility), the social standing retained (0 to 1, where 1 means nobody criticised), the aggregate utility $U = E \cdot S$, and the utility per unit of gross revenue, which is what social learning compares.

Spatial attributes include a unique identifier for the prefecture, the name of the province to which the prefecture belongs, and the spatial geometry of the prefecture boundary. Prefecture-level agents also have dynamic variables for irrigation area, water use intensity, and a dynamic quota variable indexed by year. More information can be found in Table S3.

##### Spatial and Temporal Scales

The model covers the Yellow River Basin, encompassing 8 provinces and 59 prefecture-level cities. The spatial resolution is 0.1 degrees (about 11 km at the equator, or 121 km² per cell). The temporal extent spans from 1980 to 2012 (33 years) with annual time steps, of which **1980 is a spin-up year that every analysis discards**, leaving the 1981-2012 analysis window. The spin-up is needed because an agent's payoff is computed from the crop yield that the previous step simulated: in the first simulated year that yield does not exist yet, so gross revenue is zero, the economic payoff reduces to minus the water bill, and the social term vanishes identically. That year therefore carries no behavioural information and is cut off when results are read. The strict enforcement regime begins in 1998, which corresponds to year 19 of the simulation and serves as the policy intervention point. The window ends in 2012 because the "strictest water resources management" system introduced after it changed the administrative incentives prefecture-level agencies face, and the model does not represent that regime. The end year is also the last one the data support on both sides: provincial quotas and irrigation intensity stop at 2013, and the water-saving-irrigation statistics used for external validation break between 2012 and 2013.

#### Process Overview and Scheduling

The model follows an annual cycle. At each time step (year), the sequence of processes begins with province-level updates. Next, prefecture-level decision-making occurs in random order. Each prefecture-level agent loads annual climate data and updates dynamic variables including irrigation area and water use intensity. Each agent then simulates crop growth using AquaCrop [@vanuytrecht2014] to estimate irrigation requirements and potential yields. Before any prefecture steps, the model freezes every agent's realised compliance from the previous year into a snapshot; that snapshot is the observation set every agent uses for peer judgement all year, so the social term is evaluated on one clean $t-1$ cross-section rather than on a mixture of updated and not-yet-updated neighbours.

Each agent enters the year already carrying the decision intention (willing) that it drew at the end of the previous year from its boldness trait, subject to policy constraints; it then solves for its water source portfolio (surface water versus groundwater) by comparing utility at the corners of the feasible set, and records actual water withdrawals and crop yields. Following decision-making, social learning occurs in random order. Each prefecture-level agent compares its performance with connected neighbours, using utility **per unit of gross revenue** so that the comparison does not simply track prefecture size. Agents may adopt behavioural traits (b and v, see Appendix S1) from better-performing neighbours and may mutate their strategy with a small probability (parameter u, also see Appendix S1).

### Design Concepts

#### Basic Principles

The model is grounded in two theoretical frameworks. Evolutionary game theory provides the foundation: agents play a common-pool resource game in which complying with the water quota is cooperation and breaching it is defection, and the social payoff makes each agent's utility depend on what its neighbours did as well as on what it did itself. The Plural Rationality Approach (PRA) then supplies the social cost, which it derives from two cultural dimensions, individualism/collectivism and norm valuation. The boldness and vengefulness traits carry those two dimensions into the model.

**Where the two parameters come from.** The Grid and Group values entering the social standing function are taken from the World Values Survey (WVS) [@chai2009]. Grid (`s_grid`) defaults to a single national constant of 0.39: the survey's `Grid score` column min-max scaled across its 60 countries, read off China's entry as $(0.56-0.40)/(0.81-0.40)$. The scaling is required rather than cosmetic, because the column spans 0.40–0.81 while eq. (3) needs a share; being monotone, it also settles the older "invert or not" argument by making it moot.

**Where the ordering across space comes from.** Grid can be made spatially heterogeneous through the same mapping used for Group,
$\text{Grid}_i = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\overline{\text{Grid}} + \kappa_\text{grid}\, t_i,\ \varepsilon,\ 1-\varepsilon\right)$
(config keys `grid_bar`, `kappa_grid`), in which $t_i$ is a standardised cultural-tightness index [@chua2019] covering 11,662 respondents across 31 provinces on Gelfand's six-item scale. These data do not identify the national **level** of tightness at all: Chua's index is standardised within China's 31 provinces, so its mean is zero by construction. We therefore use the WVS `Grid score` value (min-max scaled, 0.39) as a **proxy** for that level, and state plainly what the proxy does and does not claim. It is not the same instrument as Gelfand tightness. Gelfand et al. [@gelfand2011] place China on the tight side, whereas the WVS column places it at the 16.7th percentile, and we do not assert that the two agree.

What justifies the substitution is that the model barely responds to the level across the whole plausible range. Raising `grid_bar` from 0.39 to 0.70 lifts the sanctioning rate from 39% to 70% and the single-year deterrent from 2.84× to 6.50×, but the economic temptation has a median of 1.11 and a 90th percentile of 1.64, so both anchors sit far above it and the static compliance share moves only from 0.973 to 0.993. The division of labour is therefore explicit: **the World Values Survey supplies the level, on which the results barely depend, and Chua et al. supply the ordering across provinces, on which every spatial conclusion rests.** Saturation describes a single year's judgement, while the simulated model compounds small per-year edges over three decades of imitation, so we treat `grid_bar` as a sensitivity parameter rather than as a measured quantity.

**Resolution.** Tightness is measured at province level while the agents are prefectures, so `City.s_grid_level` selects the resolution: `national` (the constant above), `province` (each agent takes its own province's value), or `prefecture` (that value plus a draw from the standard error of the province mean, which propagates the survey's own uncertainty to the agent grain rather than inventing data; the draw uses a private generator seeded by `City.s_grid_draw_seed` and never perturbs the model's own random stream). The reported results use `province` with $\kappa_\text{grid} = 0.10$. We implemented and measured the `prefecture` level and do **not** use it: at the calibrated dispersion the within-province spread of `Grid` reaches only about ±0.03, too small to move the reporting threshold, and a placebo test finds no detectable within-province signal (partial correlation +0.24 against a placebo of +0.25). Setting `s_grid_level` to `national` restores the homogeneous-Grid model bit for bit, since that branch returns before any file is read.

**Why the sign of `kappa_grid` is an identity rather than a convention.** The enforcement share is exactly `Grid`, since an agent reports only when $v > 1-\text{Grid}$ and $v\sim U(0,1)$. `Grid` therefore *is* the fraction of eligible neighbours who sanction a defector, which is what the second clause of the tightness definition measures: *the extent to which people are punished or sanctioned when they deviate*. The cost channel agrees independently, because a report burns $1-\text{Grid}$ of goodwill, and where sanctioning deviance is expected the critic bears no stigma, so tight provinces face the low cost and hold the high `Grid`. Conceptually Grid measures **how far rules are externally imposed**: where Grid is low the rules are nobody's business but your own, so reporting a neighbour is a personal betrayal that earns the informer's name, whereas where Grid is high, roles and rules come from outside the individual, reporting is merely doing one's part, and a high-Grid society follows norms strictly and is *more* willing to punish breaches of them even with no direct benefit to the punisher.

Grid is therefore the share of goodwill that **survives** each report, its complement $1-\text{Grid}$ is the per-report cost, and it acts on the **decision to enforce**: an agent reports a defecting neighbour only when $v_i>1-\text{Grid}$, so the share of eligible agents who enforce is exactly $\text{Grid}$. A larger Grid therefore buys cheaper, more frequent enforcement and *stronger* peer deterrence. Enforcement is the *only* route by which Grid reaches the compliance decision.

**What cancels, and why.** An agent that is itself breaching has no standing to criticise, but its realised behaviour in year $t-1$ decides that, not the candidate decision being scored: what it intends this year cannot change whether it complied last year. The number of reports it files, $m$, is therefore identical in both branches. Writing $d$ for those reports and $c$ for the criticisms it would attract while defecting, $S(\text{comply}) = \text{Grid}^{d}$ and $S(\text{defect}) = \text{Grid}^{d}(1-\text{Group})^{c}$, so $\text{Grid}^{d}$ cancels and the agent actually compares $(1-\text{Group})^{-c}$. Two consequences follow, both properties of the eligibility rule rather than of eq. (3): the deterrent never falls below 1, so the social term can never reward defection; and Grid moves compliance only by swelling or thinning the ranks of enforcers, monotonically. We implemented and measured a variant that judges eligibility on the candidate decision (2026-08-31). It does return Grid to the ratio, at the price of inverting the deterrent on roughly 19% of the parameter plane, raising the breach rate by 2.9 pp, and making a judgement depend on which of the two agents is asked, so we rejected it. The eligibility condition itself is our addition; the source imposes none.

Both config keys hold the source's symbols **as they are**, each obtained by min-max scaling its World Values Survey column across the 60 countries and reading off China: $\text{Grid} = $ `s_grid` $= 0.39$ and $\text{Group} = $ `s_group` $= 0.47$. Group measures **how far the individual is embedded in the collective**: under low Group everyone minds their own business and being caught barely hurts, while under high Group individual and collective interests overlap heavily, so being *seen* by the collective to breach is enormously costly. The single complement anywhere in the social term is the one eq. (3) writes on Group, giving $S = 0.39^m\,0.53^n$; Grid needs none, because it is already the share of goodwill that survives a report. The deterrent contributed by a single critic is therefore $1/(1-\text{Group}) = 1.89$.

Group is by default the same national constant, but can be made prefecture-specific via
$\text{Group}_i = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\overline{\text{Group}} + \kappa\, z_i,\ \varepsilon,\ 1-\varepsilon\right)$
(config keys `group_bar`, `City.s_group_kappa`), where $z_i$ is the standardised prefecture collectivism index and `kappa` (`City.s_group_kappa`) controls how much heterogeneity is admitted. With $\kappa = 0$, the default, every agent receives the national constant and the model is identical to the homogeneous-culture version. The census wave used as the fixed cross-section is set by `City.s_group_wave` (default 2000, which precedes the bulk of the simulated policy period). Third, agents are boundedly rational utility maximisers. They optimise their water source portfolio within constraints but do not have perfect information or computational capacity. Fourth, the model explicitly couples human decision-making (social subsystem) with biophysical processes (natural subsystem through AquaCrop), enabling feedback between irrigation decisions and crop productivity.

#### Emergence

System-level patterns emerge from individual agent decisions. When surface water quotas are strictly enforced, agents shift to groundwater to meet irrigation needs. This groundwater substitution emerges with spatial heterogeneity in compliance that some provinces (e.g., Shandong, Ningxia, Neimeng, Henan, and Gansu) consistently exceeding quotas while others comply. Efficiency improvements emerge as agents face stricter constraints and optimise their water use. This improvement emerges from the optimization process rather than being explicitly programmed. Finally, productivity gains emerge despite reduced surface water availability.

#### Adaptation

The agent includes two key adaptation mechanisms: adapting to climate change and adapting to policy changes. Climate change affects the volume of water potentially needed for irrigation, while policy changes affect the amount of water available for irrigation. The agent adapts by adjusting the components of water use.

#### Objectives

Prefecture-level agents seek to maximise their aggregate utility $U_{i,t}=E_{i,t}\cdot S_{i,t}$ (i.e., Appendix S1, Equation 5), where $E_{i,t}$ is the economic payoff (crop revenue minus water abstraction costs, in CNY) and $S_{i,t}\in[0,1]$ is the social standing that survives peer judgement. Both enter on their **raw** values: an earlier formulation ranked each term within the agent's network before multiplying, which made the comparison depend on the composition of the network rather than on the agent's own behaviour, and has been removed.

#### Learning

Social learning occurs at the end of each year, when agents compare their performance with connected neighbours. The comparison uses utility per unit of gross revenue, $U_{i,t}/R_{i,t}$, not absolute utility: prefectures differ in irrigated area by an order of magnitude, so comparing absolute payoffs would select traits by size rather than by behaviour, and the population would converge on whichever trait the largest prefecture happened to start with. Agents with no harvest that year have no performance to imitate and are neither copied nor copiers. If neighbours perform better, agents adopt the neighbours' behavioural traits, allowing successful strategies to diffuse through the network. Strategy mutation occurs with a small probability, where agents randomly reset either boldness or vengefulness to a random value in [0, 1]. This prevents the population from converging to local optima and maintains behavioural diversity. Learning occurs annually, allowing agents to respond to changing conditions such as policy enforcement and climate variability. Also see Appendix S1, Equation (7).

#### Prediction

Agents use the AquaCrop crop model to predict crop yields and irrigation requirements. For each prefecture $i$ and crop $c$ (maize, wheat, rice) in year $t$, AquaCrop provides the irrigation water requirement $W_{i,c,t}^{req}$ and the realized yield $Y_{i,c,t}$ as a function of local climate, soils, and crop parameters (see Appendix S1, Equation 9). Agents do not predict future climate or policy changes; they make decisions based on current-year conditions and historical learning.

#### Sensing

Agents sense several types of information as summarised in Appendix S1 Table S3.

#### Interaction

Interactions occur through several mechanisms. Direct interactions occur through the social network, where prefecture-level agents observe each other's payoffs directly — the comparison is on the raw utility, not on ranks. Agents observe neighbours' decisions, performance, and traits, and agents' social payoffs depend on neighbours' compliance behaviour. Indirect interactions occur through institutional mechanisms, as quota allocation creates competition among prefectures within the same province. Policy enforcement affects all agents simultaneously. Environmental feedback occurs as agents' water withdrawal decisions affect crop yields through AquaCrop, and crop yields feed back into economic payoffs, influencing future decisions.

#### Stochasticity

Several processes involve randomness. During initialisation, boldness and vengefulness are initialised as random values in [0, 1] for each prefecture-level agent, and the initial willing decision is determined randomly based on boldness. Social network formation involves each Province agent creating links between prefecture-level agents with probability $l_p$, and the network structure varies each year (recreated annually). In decision-making, the willing decision is stochastic: willing equals "D" with probability boldness, otherwise "C" — and it is now the model's only per-agent source of randomness inside a year, because the portfolio solve is exact and peer judgement is a deterministic threshold rule. In social learning, when multiple better-performing neighbours exist, selection can be random, and strategy mutation occurs with probability. Social cost calculation no longer involves random draws: whether an agent criticises a defecting neighbour is the deterministic rule $v_i>1-\text{Grid}$, so both sides of a pair necessarily agree on whether the report happened. Stochasticity ensures that the model can explore multiple pathways and results are not deterministic. Multiple runs are used to assess robustness; the reported results use 50 replicates per scenario (`exp.repeats=50`, recorded in the canonical run's `multirun.yaml`).

#### Collectives

Agents form no groups of their own. The only collective in the model is the social
network a prefecture belongs to: each agent evaluates its own payoff on raw values
(Appendix S1, Equation 5) and then imitates the best-performing neighbour in that
network (Appendix S1, Equation 7). No ranking is formed over agents at any point.

#### Observation

The model collects data for analysis at multiple levels. Agent-level variables are collected annually for each prefecture-level agent, including water use variables (quota, surface water, ground water, total water use, net irrigation), agricultural variables (irrigation area by crop, actual crop yield by crop, potential crop yield by crop), performance variables (economic payoff, social payoff, aggregate utility), and behavioural variables (boldness, vengefulness, willing, decision). Aggregated outputs include basin-scale totals (sum across all prefectures), province-level aggregates, crop-specific aggregates, and efficiency indicators (calculated post-simulation). All variables are collected at the end of each time step (year), stored in CSV format for post-simulation analysis, and automatically recorded by the model's datacollector for all specified agent variables.

One consequence needs stating because it is easy to miss: the intention columns (`willing` and `actual_willing`) are re-drawn in the final statement of the step, whereas collection happens after the whole step, so the intention recorded on row $t$ is the one that governs year $t+1$ — it is **lagged by one year relative to every other column on the same row**. Pairing it with the same row's realised `decision` manufactures a category that cannot occur (an agent that intended to comply yet breached: 9.14% of post-policy agent-years in the canonical product, and exactly 0% once aligned, as `decide_boundaries` caps the upper bound at the quota for a compliant intention). Analyses must realign before pairing; the single implementation is `water_quota_analysis.analysis.indicators.align_intent_to_year`, which refuses to guess when the panel cannot be aligned.

### Details

#### Initialisation

The model is initialised in a specific sequence. First, the model class is instantiated with configuration parameters, and set up the spatial environment. Second, the spatial environment is initialised by loading prefecture boundaries from a shapefile and applying irrigation area and soil type raster data to grid cells if available. Third, prefecture-level agents are created from the shapefile, with each agent assigned a unique identifier and province name from the shapefile attributes, and each agent's spatial geometry stored. Fourth, prefecture-level agents are linked into a social network that is fully connected **within each province** and has no edges across provinces ($l_p = 1.0$). The network is rebuilt each year but never changes: social learning copies a neighbour's traits, it does not rewire the graph.

Fifth, prefecture-level agents are initialised by loading dynamic variables including water use intensity by crop type and irrigation area by crop type from CSV files indexed by year. Behavioural traits are initialised with boldness and vengefulness set to random values in [0, 1], and willing determined stochastically based on boldness. Water-related variables are initialised to 0.0, and performance metrics are initialised with economic payoff set to 0.0, social payoff set to 1.0, and aggregate utility set to 0.0. Sixth, Provinces are initialised by loading the dynamic quota variable from a CSV file indexed by year, loading water prices and crop prices from CSV files, and loading irrigation efficiency coefficients from parameters. Seventh, time is initialised with the simulation start year set to 1980 and the time step counter initialised. The model is ready to begin the first time step after initialisation.

#### Input Data

The model requires several categories of input data. Spatial data includes prefecture boundaries from a shapefile of prefecture-level administrative units, irrigation area from raster data indicating irrigated areas, and soil type from raster data of soil classifications (optional, defaults to loam). Climate data comes from the China Meteorological Forcing Dataset (CMFD) [@he2020b], including daily precipitation in millimetres, maximum and minimum temperature in degrees Celsius, and reference evapotranspiration in millimetres (spatial resolution is 0.1 degrees, temporal coverage spans 1980-2018, of which 1980-2012 is used). In the ABM the format is pre-processed CSV files, one per prefecture.

Institutional data includes annual provincial quotas from the "87 Water Allocation Scheme" in a CSV file indexed by province and year, and the policy enforcement year is 1998 (when the hard constraint begins). Economic data includes water prices (surface water and groundwater prices by province in a CSV file) and crop prices (rice, wheat, and maize prices by province from the China Agricultural Price Survey Yearbook in a CSV file). Water prices are in CNY per cubic metre, and crop prices are in CNY per tonne.

Agricultural data includes annual irrigation area by crop type and prefecture in a CSV file indexed by prefecture and year (units: hectares) and annual water use intensity by crop type and prefecture in a CSV file indexed by prefecture and year (units: millimetres), sourced from the national water resources survey and statistical yearbooks.

Technical parameters include the provincial conveyance efficiency coefficients for surface water and groundwater (from literature; these cover losses between the source and the field only, and range from 0.40 to 0.89), the field application efficiency `AppEff` (90%, applied by AquaCrop to every irrigation event and covering losses between application and the root zone), and AquaCrop parameters for rice, wheat, and maize (calibrated against provincial yield statistics).

Cultural data includes a prefecture-level collectivism index in a CSV file indexed by prefecture (`ds.city_collectivism`), derived from the 2000/2010/2020 population censuses and used to give each prefecture-level agent its own Group parameter (see *Role of Social Norms and Cultural Values*). This file is read only when the heterogeneity parameter is non-zero. A province-level cultural-tightness table (`ds.city_tightness`, aligned to the same agents) plays the same role for the Grid parameter and is read only when `City.s_grid_level` is not `national` and `City.s_grid_kappa` is non-zero. All data files are specified in the configuration file. Missing data for specific provinces or crops are handled by the model (e.g., some provinces lack rice price data).

#### Submodels

The Crop-Water Quota Irrigation Model (CWatQIM) integrates a crop growth model (AquaCrop) as the natural subsystem and an agent-based model (ABM) as the social subsystem. AquaCrop, developed by the UN Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) [@steduto2009], simulates crops yield under different climate and irrigation conditions, balancing accuracy, simplicity, and robustness. See Appendix S1 for more details about the two submodels.

Water reaches the crop through two efficiency layers, applied at different points and by different parameters. First, **conveyance**: the water use intensity $\text{WUI}_{i,c,t}$ read from irrigation statistics is the gross withdrawal at the source, and multiplying it by the provincial coefficients $\eta_{sw}$ / $\eta_{gw}$ (0.40--0.89, higher for groundwater in every province) gives the depth arriving at the field. This depth enters AquaCrop as the seasonal cap `MaxIrrSeason` on cumulative applied irrigation. Second, **field application**: AquaCrop applies $\text{AppEff}=90\%$ — the value `City.irr_eff` carries in the configuration — to each irrigation event, inflating the requested depth by $(200-\text{AppEff})/100=1.1$ when deciding how much to apply and infiltrating $\text{AppEff}/100=90\%$ of what was applied. The parameter was swept over 50%, 70% and 90%; the choice moves the fit but not the conclusions. The two layers compose rather than duplicate, so water entering the root zone is about $\text{WUI}\cdot\eta\cdot\text{AppEff}/100$. Because $\eta_{gw}>\eta_{sw}$ everywhere, substituting groundwater raises the delivered depth for the same gross withdrawal, which is the pathway linking source substitution to the efficiency and productivity indicators.

### Decision-Making

#### Decision Subjects and Objects

Prefecture-level agents (prefecture-level water management agencies) are the primary decision-makers in the model. The main decision is the allocation of irrigation water between surface water and groundwater sources. Specifically, agents decide how much surface water to withdraw ($W_s$), how much groundwater to withdraw ($W_g$), and whether to comply with or violate the surface water quota ($Q_i$). Decision-making occurs at the individual agent level (prefecture-level). There is no collective decision-making; each agent makes independent decisions based on its own constraints and objectives.

#### Basic Rationality

Agents are boundedly rational utility maximisers. They seek to maximise their aggregate payoff ($U$), have limited information (only know their own and neighbours' states), solve their portfolio choice exactly rather than heuristically — the objective is piecewise affine, so comparing the corners is both cheaper and more accurate than any search — make decisions based on current-year conditions (no forward-looking behaviour), and adapt behaviour through social learning (backward-looking). This bounded rationality reflects real-world constraints: water managers cannot perfectly predict future conditions, have limited computational resources, and must make decisions under uncertainty.

#### Objectives and Success Criteria

The primary objective is to maximise aggregate utility $U_i = E_i \cdot S_i$: the economic return is discounted by the social standing that survives peer judgement, following the multiplicative form used by the study whose social sub-model and World Values Survey calibration we adopt [@castillarho2017a]. The economic component ($E_i$) involves maximizing crop revenue ($c(P_c\cdot Y_c\cdot A_c)$) and minimizing water costs ($(C_s\cdot W_s+C_g\cdot W_g)$), resulting in a net economic payoff that can be negative. The social component ($S_i$) involves maximizing compliance-related satisfaction, avoiding reputational costs from non-compliance, and avoiding social dissatisfaction from others' non-compliance, with a range from 0 (maximum dissatisfaction) to 1 (full satisfaction). Success criteria involve agents comparing their utility **per unit of gross revenue** with neighbours, where a higher value indicates better performance; the normalisation keeps the comparison from simply tracking prefecture size. Agents learn from better-performing neighbours. There is no explicit threshold for "success"; performance is relative.

#### Decision Process

The decision-making process occurs in several stages.

**Stage 1** is the preliminary intention decision. The intention that governs year $t$ is drawn at the **end of year $t-1$**, in the last statement of the agent's step, and is held on the agent until it is consumed the following year (`City.step`). It is therefore already fixed when year $t$ begins; nothing between the draw and its use changes boldness, so drawing it a step earlier is distributionally equivalent to drawing it at the start of year $t$ — but the recorded timing is the one described here, not the latter. If the policy is strictly enforced (year >= 1998 in the strict scenario), agents are forced to comply (willing equals "C"). Otherwise, agents stochastically choose based on boldness: the probability of choosing "D" equals boldness. This intention determines the feasible region for the solve but does not guarantee the final decision: because the compliant corner lies inside the "D" branch's feasible set, the solve may find that compliance is optimal even for an agent that intended to defect. Boldness is therefore a **propensity**, and the realised breach rate is strictly below it.

**Stage 2** is boundary condition determination. Based on willing, the agent determines the upper bound for surface water withdrawal. If willing equals "C", surface water is constrained by quota, so the upper bound equals the minimum of quota and total irrigation demand. If willing equals "D", surface water can equal total demand in the baseline scenario. In the strict enforcement scenario (post-1998), the hard constraint $W_s\le Q_i$ is always enforced regardless of willing.

**Stage 3** is the portfolio solve. Given a candidate $W_s$, the agent sets $W_g=W_{total}-W_s$, values the harvest with the crop yields carried over from the **previous** year's AquaCrop run — they are bound once before the solve and do not respond to the candidate, so within one year the harvest revenue is a constant and only water cost and the social term vary — and returns the utility

$$U_{i,t} = E_{i,t} \cdot S_{i,t},$$

where $E$ is crop revenue minus water costs and $S\in[0,1]$ is the social standing the agent **retains** (1 when nobody criticised). Criticism therefore discounts the whole return rather than subtracting a priced share of it, and the objective carries **no free weight parameter**: nothing in the social term is calibrated on model output. Before the policy year the social term is switched off and the objective is $U_{i,t}=E_{i,t}$ exactly.

Standing itself is equation (3) of the reference study's Supplementary Methods, reproduced unchanged:

$$S_{i,t} = \text{Grid}^{\,m}\,(1-\text{Group})^{\,n},$$

with $m$ the number of neighbours this agency criticises and $n$ the number who criticise it. The two bases run in opposite directions by design: Grid is the share of goodwill that *survives* each report filed, so sanctioning is cheap where Grid is high, while Group is the share of standing *lost* each time a breach is seen, so reputation binds hardest where Group is high. Both World Values Survey columns therefore enter directly, as the source's own Grid and Group; the only complement in the formula is the $1-\text{Group}$ written above.

This objective is **piecewise affine** in $W_s$: revenue is constant, water cost is linear, and the social term takes only two values, stepping once at the quota. Its maximum therefore lies at an endpoint, and the agent evaluates the candidate set $\{0,\ Q_{i,t},\ W_{ub}\}$ and takes the best — an exact, deterministic solve that draws no random numbers. Earlier versions searched the same interval with differential evolution; that was both unnecessary and less exact, leaving a median relative residual of 0.0009 between its answer and the corner it was approaching. Ties go to the smaller $W_s$, consistent with the strict inequality that defines a breach.

Because the compliant corner $W_s=Q_{i,t}$ lies inside the feasible set of the "D" branch, the solve can — and often does — overturn the intention: an agent that intended to defect complies whenever the reputational discount outweighs the water-cost saving. With the saving written $\Delta=(C_g-C_s)(W_{total}-Q)$, the agent defects when $\Delta\cdot S(\text{defect}) > E(\text{comply})\,[S(\text{comply})-S(\text{defect})]$ — equivalently, when the economic temptation $E(\text{defect})/E(\text{comply})$ exceeds the deterrent ratio $S(\text{comply})/S(\text{defect})$.

#### Decision Rules

Quota constraints differ by scenario. In the baseline scenario, the constraint is soft (violation incurs social cost but is allowed). In the strict enforcement scenario (post-1998), the hard constraint $W_s\le Q_i$ is enforced in optimization bounds. The water balance constraint $W_s+W_g=W_{total}$ is always enforced, where $W_{total}$ is determined exogenously from statistical data (irrigation area times water use intensity). Non-negativity constraints $W_s\geq0$ and $W_g\geq0$ are enforced in optimization bounds.

#### Adaptation to Changing Conditions

Agents adapt their behaviour in response to several factors. Policy changes occur when strict enforcement begins in 1998, forcing agents to comply (willing equals "C"). This changes the feasible region, leading to different optimal solutions, and agents may shift to groundwater to compensate for reduced surface water. Climate variability affects crop water requirements through AquaCrop, and agents adjust their water source portfolio to meet varying demands. There is no explicit climate prediction; decisions are based on current-year conditions. Social learning allows agents to observe neighbours' performance and adapt traits. Successful strategies (high payoff) spread through the network, creating spatial contagion in compliance behaviour. Economic conditions change when water prices or crop prices change (though in this study, are exogenous and input data is static).

#### Role of Social Norms and Cultural Values

Social norms are modelled through the PRA framework. Norm valuation is captured by vengefulness, which represents how much agents value compliance norms. High vengefulness means agents strongly dislike non-compliance, while low vengefulness means agents are tolerant of non-compliance. Individualism versus collectivism is captured by boldness, which represents individual risk-taking versus collective compliance. High boldness means agents are more likely to defect, while low boldness means agents prefer compliance. Social pressure is enabled by the network structure, where agents observe neighbours' behaviour and adjust their social payoffs accordingly.

Non-compliance becomes less costly when many neighbours also defect — both because fewer neighbours retain the standing to criticise, and because criticism is itself a decision. An agent files a report only when the norm matters to it more than the report costs, which reduces to $v_i > 1-\text{Grid}$; with $v\sim U(0,1)$ the share of eligible agents who enforce is exactly $\text{Grid}$. This is where the Grid dimension acts: a **higher** Grid means reporting is cheaper, hence more frequent, hence stronger deterrence. At the national anchor ($\text{Grid} = 0.39$) about 39% of eligible prefectures enforce, so horizontal peer sanctioning is active without being universal, leaving room for the vertical administrative channel that the "strict" scenario represents. That separation also answers the objection that tight societies sanction through authority rather than through peers: the vertical channel is represented **separately**, by `forced_since`, so `Grid` carries only the horizontal one and the two are not in competition for the same variance. Provinces vary around the anchor with tightness, from about 0.28 in the loosest to about 0.52 in the tightest. Cultural transmission occurs as traits (boldness and vengefulness) are transmitted through social learning, allowing norms to evolve over time. Successful strategies (high utility) become more common.

#### Spatial Aspects

Spatial aspects influence decisions through several mechanisms. Climate heterogeneity means each prefecture has its own climate time series, climate affects crop water requirements through AquaCrop, and spatial variation in climate creates heterogeneity in irrigation needs. Administrative boundaries create a hierarchical structure where prefectures belong to provinces, quota allocation occurs within provinces, and social networks are fully connected within provinces, reflecting that government performance is publicly reported and easily comparable. Spatial aggregation means decisions are made at the prefecture level (aggregated across space), there is no explicit spatial interaction beyond social networks, and irrigation area and water use are aggregated to the prefecture level.

#### Temporal Aspects

Temporal aspects play important roles. Annual time steps mean decisions are made annually (matching agricultural and administrative cycles), climate data varies annually affecting water requirements, and quota allocations may change annually from time series. Policy timing means strict enforcement begins in 1998 (year 19), creating a clear before/after comparison for policy analysis, and agents adapt to policy changes within the same year. Learning timing means social learning occurs at the end of each year (after decisions are made), agents observe outcomes before adapting, and this creates a lag between policy change and behavioural adaptation. Historical memory means agents do not explicitly remember past years, learning is based on current-year performance only, but trait evolution through learning creates implicit memory.

#### Uncertainty

Uncertainty is incorporated through several mechanisms. Stochastic decision-making now has a single source: the willing decision is a Bernoulli draw with probability boldness. The portfolio solve is exact and draws nothing, and peer judgement is a deterministic threshold rule ($v_i>1-\text{Grid}$) rather than a coin flip — so the frozen-variate bookkeeping an earlier version required is no longer needed and has been removed. Strategy mutation means random mutation maintains diversity and prevents convergence to local optima. Model uncertainty means AquaCrop model predictions have uncertainty (not explicitly modelled), parameter uncertainty (e.g., irrigation efficiency) is not propagated, and multiple model runs (replicates) are used to assess robustness. Agents do not explicitly account for uncertainty in their decisions, and they do not look ahead: the optimisation uses the previous year's realised yields, so it contains no prediction of how the current choice would change the harvest. Given this paper's focus on institutional-level impacts, we conducted a sensitivity analysis on several key parameters (mutation rate $u$, see Appendix S1, Equation 8; group and grid, see Appendix S1, Figure S2), while most of the remaining parameters are exogenous (summarised in Appendix S1, Table S3).
